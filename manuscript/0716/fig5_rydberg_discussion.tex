\subsection{Completing partially observed atom-array measurements}

Every experiment on a programmable atom array returns incomplete data: atoms
are lost from the trap, and detection assigns no outcome at the affected
sites. The standard response is to discard incomplete shots, which becomes
prohibitive for observables that involve many sites at once. Because
Diffushadow models the joint distribution of a measurement record rather than
any single observable, it can instead \emph{complete} a shot: the surviving
atoms are held fixed and the missing sites are filled in by conditional
denoising, with no retraining and no change of architecture.

We test this on a chain of $N=32$ atoms at $R_b/a = 2.35$, using held-out
ground-state snapshots that the model has never seen. A random $30\%$ of the
atoms are discarded in every shot, a loss rate far above that of current
hardware, and the model completes them conditioned on the atoms that survived
in that same shot. Figure~\ref{fig:rydberg}a shows twelve such shots before
and after completion. Two decodings are reported throughout, because they
answer different questions: sampling from the learned conditional
distribution, which is the correct choice when the goal is an ensemble
average, and most-likely decoding, which is the correct choice when the goal
is to reconstruct one particular shot.

The completed ensembles reproduce the physics of the underlying state. The
density-density structure factor retains its $Z_3$ peak at $q = 2\pi/3$ with
amplitude $0.536$, against $0.539$ for the intact data
(Fig.~\ref{fig:rydberg}b), and the spread of the excited-atom number per shot
is $0.538$ against $0.521$. Completions also respect the Rydberg blockade,
which the model was never told about: among roughly $5.5\times10^4$
nearest-neighbour pairs straddling the boundary between surviving and
completed sites, fewer than $0.02\%$ are doubly excited. A control that
generates from the detuning alone and splices the result into the lost sites
--- carrying the correct density, but blind to the surviving atoms of that
shot --- reproduces none of this: its structure-factor peak is $0.309$, its
number spread $1.168$, and it violates the blockade in up to $3.7\%$ of seam
pairs. What the model adds is therefore conditioning on the specific shot,
not merely knowledge of the phase.

The practical gain is statistical. An observable involving $k$ sites can be
evaluated only on shots in which all $k$ survive, a fraction $(1-f)^k$ of the
data at loss rate $f$. Completion returns every shot. For a $k$-site
occupation correlator spaced by the $Z_3$ period, completion recovers the
intact-data value to within $0.002$ while raising the usable sample from
$467$ to $1000$ shots at $k=2$, and from $28$ to $1000$ at $k=8$ --- a
$6.1\times$ improvement in precision at fixed experimental cost.

Finally, we ask how good the completions can possibly be.
Because the reference state is available as a matrix product state, the exact
conditional distribution of the lost sites given the surviving ones can be
computed shot by shot, which fixes an information-theoretic ceiling: no
procedure, however sophisticated, can exceed it from the same data.
Figure~\ref{fig:rydberg}c compares the model against that ceiling across the
phase diagram. The model sits on it at every detuning tested, including three
values beyond the range it was trained on. The margin over a site-wise guess
--- predicting each lost site from the mean density alone --- is zero where
the crystal is frozen and the state is effectively deterministic, and opens
to $10$ percentage points above $\delta/\Omega \approx 4.7$, where an
additional excitation enters the chain and delocalises. In that regime the
identity of the occupied sublattice varies from shot to shot, so the lost
atoms are genuinely informative about one another, and the model extracts
$99\%$ of the available information.

\subsection*{Using the model}

The three settings in this work correspond to three ways of using the same
trained network, and they carry different evidentiary weight. First, as a
\emph{surrogate sampler} at the sizes it was trained on: the model replaces a
measurement campaign, generating snapshots from which any observable is
estimated afterwards, without retraining per observable. Second, as a
\emph{cross-scale generator}: measurements are generated at system sizes
never seen in training and, through recursive extrapolation, used to estimate
thermodynamic-limit quantities. This is the strongest claim in the paper and
also the most fragile one; we therefore validate every rung against a
quasi-exact reference and state explicitly the parameter windows in which the
extrapolation is reliable. Third, as a \emph{conditional completer} of
incomplete records, which is the mode closest to experimental practice and
the one demonstrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:rydberg}.

Two properties of the architecture make the third mode possible. The
denoising process conditions on an arbitrary subset of observed tokens, so
the pattern of missing sites need not be contiguous or known in advance; and
generation proceeds one site per denoising step, so correlations between
sites completed within the same shot are preserved. An autoregressive model
with a fixed site ordering can condition only on a prefix, and cannot fill an
interleaved pattern of gaps without retraining for each pattern.

The completion results are claims about the measurement distribution at a
fixed system size, not about scaling: they show that the model has learned
the conditional structure of the state, not that it can extrapolate it. We
also note what Fig.~\ref{fig:rydberg}c does \emph{not} claim. The ceiling it
is compared against is computed from the tensor-network description of the
state, so the figure demonstrates optimality \emph{given measurement data
alone} --- the model never sees the state or the Hamiltonian --- rather than
an advantage over tensor-network methods. Likewise, the margin over the
site-wise guess exists because $N=32$ cannot host a commensurate $Z_3$
crystal; on a commensurate chain ($N=33$) the margin falls to $0.07$
percentage points. The regime in which completion is most valuable is
therefore the one experiments actually occupy --- finite arrays with
imperfect filling --- but it is a property of the array, not of the phase.

\subsection{Rydberg chain and the completion protocol}
The Rydberg Hamiltonian is
$H = \frac{\Omega}{2}\sum_i \sigma^x_i - \delta\sum_i n_i
+ \sum_{i<j} \frac{C_6}{|i-j|^6 a^6} n_i n_j$
on a chain of $N=32$ sites with open boundaries and blockade radius
$R_b/a = 2.35$, truncated at fifth-neighbour range. Ground states and
reference snapshots are obtained by DMRG; occupation measurements are
$Z$-basis readouts, tokenized exactly as the classical-shadow records used
elsewhere in this work, so no change to the model is required. Diffushadow is
trained on snapshots at $N = 16, 24, 32$ and evaluated on $1000$ held-out
shots per detuning generated with an independent seed. Atom loss is applied
with a fixed per-(detuning, loss-rate) mask, so that the two decodings are
scored on identical hidden sites. Completion conditions on the surviving
sites and denoises the remainder one site per step. The exact conditional
marginals used in Fig.~\ref{fig:rydberg}c are obtained by conditioning the
matrix product state on the surviving atoms of each shot; with nothing
observed they reproduce the DMRG density profile to $10^{-16}$.
